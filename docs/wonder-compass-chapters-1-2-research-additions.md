## Best additional evidence

### 1. Savoring: a larger evidence base for keeping hold of good experience

Chen, P.-H., Tung, H.-H., Wu, Y.-C., & Sie, J. (2026). *Effectiveness of savoring interventions: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.* Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being. [Full paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/aphw.70134).

Twenty randomized trials (4,805 participants) favored savoring overall: g = 0.51, 95% CI 0.26–0.77. There was substantial heterogeneity (I² = 86.61%), six high-risk trials, mostly self-report outcomes and a median duration of 14 days. Benefits were smaller against active controls, but that subgroup estimate remained positive; some outcomes were inconclusive.

**Use:** support the Chapter 1 savoring invitation, without a universal memory deadline or guaranteed benefit.

### 2. SkillJoy: a particularly close experimental match to several Compass practices

LaFreniere, L. S., & Newman, M. G. (2023). *Upregulating positive emotion in generalized anxiety disorder: A randomized controlled trial of the SkillJoy ecological momentary intervention.* Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology. [Original study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36716146/), DOI 10.1037/ccp0000794.

Eighty-five participants diagnosed with GAD received seven days of smartphone exercises or active self-monitoring. Exercises included noticing positive experience, planning enjoyable activities, and recording and reflecting on good events. Post-intervention positive emotion and worry improved more than the control. At 30 days, the clearer between-group advantage was in savoring.

**Use:** a close practical analogue, not a Compass or single-sentence test. It is included in the review above, not additional independent evidence beyond that review.

### 3. Enjoying familiar things differently: direct support for the playful mission

O'Brien, E., & Smith, R. W. (2019; online 2018). *Unconventional Consumption Methods and Enjoying Things Consumed: Recapturing the “First-Time” Experience.* Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin. [Publisher](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167218779823); [author-uploaded full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325820216_Unconventional_Consumption_Methods_and_Enjoying_Things_Consumed_Recapturing_the_First-Time_Experience).

Four experiments tested popcorn, water and videos, including a preregistered video replication. Enjoyment could improve with unusual methods, but not when novelty impeded immersion; benefits could diminish with repetition.

**Use:** the popcorn illustration as a close analogue for the blue-object game, not a test of that specific game.

### 4. Vacation mindset: the closest fit to the Myth of the Mountain

West, C., Mogilner, C., & DeVoe, S. E. (2021; copyright 2020). *Happiness From Treating the Weekend Like a Vacation.* Social Psychological and Personality Science. [Author-hosted full text](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5e270ffe949f6c4d0d0fcc55/t/6050d0ac3c765f2a86b1c853/1615909036411/Happiness%2BVacations%2B-%2BSPPS%2B-%2BWest%2BMogilner%2BDeVoe.pdf), DOI 10.1177/1948550620916080.

Three preregistered experiments favored vacation framing, most consistently for Monday satisfaction; the third had smaller, mixed effects. Present attention was a proposed mediator. Spending increased in the first study, with benefits surviving adjustment; the second found no significant spending difference.

**Use:** the Chapter 2 addition. Do not claim no travel or spending. Demand effects, attrition and self-report limit inference; long-term repetition was untested.

### 5. Personal memory cues: a reason to return to a souvenir

Martin, C. B., et al. (2022). *A smartphone intervention that enhances real-world memory and promotes differentiation of hippocampal activity in older adults.* PNAS. [Original study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36512503/); [full paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366241543_A_smartphone_intervention_that_enhances_real-world_memory_and_promotes_differentiation_of_hippocampal_activity_in_older_adults), DOI 10.1073/pnas.2214285119.

Two older-adult experiments (22 and 12 participants) found repeated audiovisual cue replay improved detailed recollection versus recorded events not replayed, with benefits at three months. Positive sentiment was measured through text analysis, not proof of restored feelings.

**Use:** support revisiting souvenirs. A written sentence is an extrapolation, not the tested intervention; writing once cannot be assigned these effects.

### 6. Acceptance: useful attention is an invitation, not another performance demand

Lindsay, E. K., et al. (2018). *How mindfulness training promotes positive emotions: Dismantling acceptance skills training in two randomized controlled trials.* Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. [Original study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30550321/); [author manuscript](https://www.healthandhumanperformancelab.com/_files/ugd/b30fc8_52508d284d0f453ea14c1082bffa3e5f.pdf), DOI 10.1037/pspa0000134.

Trials with 137 and 153 stressed adults compared monitoring plus acceptance, monitoring alone and controls. Combined training produced greater gains in daily positive affect. One program lasted eight weeks, the other two; neither included long-term follow-up. These were structured mindfulness programs, not one noticing prompt.

**Use:** strengthen the manner of invitation: readers can notice without demanding an immediate good feeling. This supports retaining permission and curiosity, while avoiding “try harder to pay attention” as the whole explanation. It does not show that all attention-only exercises fail.

## Additional research worth keeping in the notes

### Small departures from routine, memory and mood

Meade, M. E., et al. (2024). *Unique events improve episodic richness, enhance mood, and alter the perception of time during isolation.* Scientific Reports. [Full paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-80591-z).

Eighteen older adults recorded 670 events during lockdown. More distinctive events were associated with richer recall, better reported mood and less boredom; examples included local exploration and new recipes. This is a small study with self-selected experiences, not a randomized test of novelty itself. Faster perceived passage of time during the day does not establish a longer-feeling life in retrospect. The shared app and research group mean it is not wholly independent corroboration of Martin's work.

**Use:** support modest micro-adventures and the time-blur discussion; keep its detailed findings out of the main narrative.

### Novelty can fit inside a familiar life

Welch, E., Schelkun, V., Gasser, C., Lockwood, K., & Davachi, L. (2025). *Rich autobiographical memory benefits from both novelty and similarity to other daily experiences.* Scientific Reports. [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-29036-9); [published PDF](https://d-nb.info/1391871561/34).

Forty-one young adults completed a two-week diary with later memory testing. Events labeled new were recalled more vividly; among new events, similarity to other experiences was associated with greater vividness and more positive ratings. This was observational and predominantly female. It supports the possibility of meaningful novelty within familiar contexts, rather than requiring maximum unfamiliarity. It does not compare Thailand with Waterville or prove why either trip was remembered differently.

**Use:** a recent source behind the local-adventure argument, without adding another research paragraph.

### Everyday variety and positive affect

Heller, A. S., et al. (2020). *Association between real-world experiential diversity and positive affect relates to hippocampal–striatal functional connectivity.* Nature Neuroscience. [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9169417/), DOI 10.1038/s41593-020-0636-4.

GPS and experience sampling in 132 young adults linked more varied daily locations to greater positive affect. Associations ran in both temporal directions. This supports exploration as a plausible contributor to a reinforcing positive pattern, not proof that a new route causes happiness or grows the hippocampus.

**Use:** background support for Embark. The more directly matched experiments above deserve the reader's limited space.

### Returning to positive memories and acute stress

Speer, M. E., & Delgado, M. R. (2017). *Reminiscing about positive memories buffers acute stress responses.* Nature Human Behaviour. [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6719713/), DOI 10.1038/s41562-017-0093.

Behavioral and imaging experiments (134 and 43 participants) found a smaller cortisol rise after an acute stressor when participants recalled positive rather than neutral memories. Some mood comparisons were less conclusive. The authors explicitly discuss difficulty accessing positive memories and uncertainty about chronic stress.

**Use:** supporting notes for revisiting good moments. This does not imply that a reader can reliably lower cortisol with a sentence or that difficulty reconnecting with joy is a failure to try.

### Appreciation as a contributor to meaning

Kim, J., et al. (2022). *Experiential appreciation as a pathway to meaning in life.* Nature Human Behaviour. [Original abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35145278/), DOI 10.1038/s41562-021-01283-6.

Across seven studies, valuing experience predicted perceived meaning beyond purpose, coherence and mattering. The original abstract and publisher summary were accessible; full methods were not verified in this pass, so this is not used for a specific experimental claim.

**Use:** a promising background source for the idea that an experience can matter without being productive. No new body text recommended on abstract-level access alone.

## Findings that prevent overstatement

A later active-control trial, Pancini et al. (2026), [*Savor-Aging*](https://doi.org/10.1111/aphw.70184), reported no significant time-by-group effects on several broad wellbeing outcomes. Publisher and repository text were partly accessible; complete methods were not verified. It should not be promoted as proof that savoring outperforms another engaging positive-emotion activity. It is a useful reminder to describe the overall evidence as promising and variable.

The strongest support concerns the practices and near-term experiences. It does not establish a universal Rut syndrome, identical physiology for every reader, or the unique effectiveness of the complete Compass sequence. None of that requires turning a field guide into a research paper: the reader can receive a concrete invitation while the notes carry the scope of the evidence.

## Review scope

This was a targeted research search, not an exhaustive systematic review. Original papers and published analyses were prioritized. Full papers or author manuscripts were inspected where accessible; selected papers required indexed original text because publisher/repository access failed. Access limits are stated above where they affect proposed use. Proposed wording is restrained to verified results and explicitly identified practical extrapolations.
