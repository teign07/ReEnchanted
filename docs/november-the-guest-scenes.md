## 1. The Knock

**id** `november.scene.the-knock` · **When** All Souls' Eve, Sun Nov 1, night · **Room** the
Kitchens · **Cast** Ambrose Trencher, the Guest

**Opening (desk line):** Somebody's knocking at the door nobody uses.

### Node `entry`

The soul cakes are cooling on the long table.

There are forty of them, flat and round and the colour of a good brown paper bag, and every one has
a cross cut into the top before it went in the oven. Ambrose makes them every All Souls. Nobody
asked him to. The old Academy did it, so he does it. He says a custom's like a pot: if nobody uses
it, it rusts.

The Kitchens are warm and nearly dark. The big fire's been banked down to a red heap. The pots hang
in their rows over the range, and the long pine table runs away into the shadows like a road. It
smells of nutmeg and hot currants and the end of a long day.

You've set me on the end of the table, by the cakes. I can smell them through my cover. Paper
remembers every kitchen, and I'm remembering this one as hard as I can.

Then somebody knocks.

It isn't the door from the Great Hall. Everybody uses that one. It isn't the yard door where the
milk comes in. It's the little door at the west end, past the pantry. It's low and black, with
iron studs, and its bolts are painted over so thick they look like knuckles. Nobody uses the
west door. Ambrose keeps a sack of onions against it.

The knock comes three times. It's polite. It's patient. It sounds like somebody who's knocked on a
great many doors.

Ambrose wipes his hands on his apron. He looks at the west door for a long moment. He looks at the
soul cakes. Then he moves the onions.

The door sticks. He has to put his shoulder to it. It opens with a sound like a stair giving way,
and the cold comes in all at once, a wet black November cold that smells of leaves and stone.

Somebody's standing on the step.

They're small. They come up to about Ambrose's elbow. They're wearing a coat that's far too big for
them, a long green coat with brass buttons, the kind a grand person might've worn to church a very
long time ago. It's gone ragged at the hem. It's dark with rain. The hood's up, and I can't see a
face under it. I can see two hands, folded in front, and they're very clean.

The somebody says, in a voice like a spoon in an empty bowl:

“A soul, a soul, a soul cake. Please, good master, a soul cake. An apple, a pear, a plum or a
cherry, any good thing to make us all merry.”

It's the old souling rhyme. Children used to go door to door with it on All Souls, a long time ago,
asking for cakes and saying a prayer for the house's dead in return. I know it from three different
books. I've never heard anybody say it out loud before. It sounds older out loud.

Ambrose doesn't ask who they are. That's the first thing I notice, and I notice it very hard.

He takes a soul cake off the tray. He puts it in their clean hands. Then he steps back from the
door, so the way's clear.

“Come and sit by the fire,” he says. “You've had a long walk.”

The somebody comes in.

They walk the way a cat walks into a new house: along the wall, and slowly, touching nothing. They
sit on the low stool by the banked fire. They eat the soul cake in small, careful bites, with the
hood still up, and they don't drop a crumb.

They don't say thank you.

Ambrose doesn't wait for it. He's already putting the kettle on.

I lean my corner towards the stool, just a little, the way I lean towards anything new. I want to
know what they smell of. Everybody smells of something. Penny smells of pencil shavings and cold
tea. Wicker smells of grass and trouble. You smell of your own house, and your soap, and the
weather you walked in.

The somebody by the fire smells of nothing at all.

It's like leaning towards a kitchen that isn't there any more.

I close my cover. Not all the way. Enough.

Ambrose brings the tea. While it's cooling, he takes a second soul cake off the tray and bites it,
the way a cook always tastes his own batch.

He stops chewing.

He looks at the cake. He looks at the stool. He doesn't say anything, but his jaw goes very still.

Later, when the somebody's asleep by the fire, he tells you very quietly what it tasted of.

“Nothing,” he says. “Like eating the shape of a cake.”

The somebody wakes before the tea's gone cold. They turn the hood towards you. I still can't see a
face. I can see the fire in two small round places where eyes would be.

“And who are you, that carries a book about?” they say. “What do they call you?”

The kettle ticks. Ambrose has gone very still at the range. In every story I've ever held, there's
a moment where somebody asks your name at a fire. I've never liked what happens next.

### Node `morning` (after any choice)

In the morning the stranger's still on the stool by the fire, asleep, with the hood up and the
green coat pulled around them like a tent.

Somebody's scrubbed every pot in the Kitchens.

All of them. The big stock pots and the little milk pans and the copper ones Ambrose saves for
jam. Somebody's scoured them till they shine, and hung them back on their hooks in a perfect row,
biggest to smallest, with their handles all pointing the same way.

Ambrose stands in front of them in his nightshirt with a cold cup of tea in his hand. He doesn't
say anything for a long time.

Then he says, “That's not how I hang them.”

He doesn't move them back.

## 2. Three Nights

**id** `november.scene.three-nights` · **When** Tue Nov 3 to Thu Nov 5, Bonfire Night · **Room**
the Kitchens, then the courtyard · **Cast** Ambrose Trencher, Dr. Vellum, Lydia Boggle, Penny
Blackletter, Professor Momort, the Guest

**Opening:** It's been three nights. The Academy's started calling them the Guest.

### Node `entry`

The stranger's been at the Academy for three nights now.

Nobody's learned their name. The students have started calling them the Guest, with a capital G,
the way you'd say the Headmistress or the Weather. The Guest sleeps on the stool by the fire. The
Guest eats whatever Ambrose puts in front of them: porridge, stew, toast, the burnt ends of things,
a whole apple core and all. The Guest never says thank you, and never refuses a second helping.

And every morning, the pots are scrubbed.

Dr. Vellum comes down to the Kitchens on the third evening with a notebook, a thermometer and a
look.

“I'd like to examine your visitor,” Vellum says. “Temperature. Pulse. Appetite. Three nights of
eating like that and nobody's taken a single measurement.”

Ambrose is chopping onions. He doesn't stop.

“No.”

“It's a simple examination.”

“They've eaten at my hearth three nights,” says Ambrose. “That makes them a guest. You don't poke a
guest with a thermometer.”

“That isn't a rule.”

“It's the oldest rule there is.”

Professor Boggle is sitting at the end of the long table, mending a tea towel. She keeps the
Academy's rooms. She knows where every key is and which stairs creak and which chimneys need a
word. She doesn't look up from her needle.

“He's right, I'm afraid,” she says. “The house rules are older than the Academy. Three nights at
the hearth and they're under its roof. Nobody can turn them out. Nobody can harm them. Not till
they choose to go.”

Vellum looks at the Guest. The Guest is sitting on the stool by the fire, eating a bowl of
porridge very slowly, with the hood up.

“And if they never choose to go?”

Nobody answers that.

### Node `bonfire`

It's Bonfire Night, and the courtyard's full.

The bonfire's taller than the stable roof. It roars and spits, and sparks go up into the black sky
like somebody's shaken out a blanket full of stars. Everybody's in scarves. Everybody's breath is
showing. Ambrose has brought out trays of parkin, sticky and dark and smelling of black treacle and
ginger, and bags of bonfire toffee you have to break with a little hammer.

The Guest stands at the very edge of the firelight.

They won't come closer. Everybody else is crowding in to the warmth, holding their hands out, and
the Guest stays back where the dark starts, in their long green coat, with a square of parkin in
their clean hands.

Penny finds Ambrose at the toffee table. She's carrying his book.

It's *The Middlemost Cookery*. He bought it at an estate sale thirty years ago and he carries it
everywhere, in his apron pocket, wrapped in a tea towel. The spine's held together with string.
He won't say what he keeps looking for in it. Penny's holding it open at the page for parkin.

“Ambrose. Read the second line.”

He reads it. His face does something slow.

The second line says *1 lb of oatmeal, ½ lb of black _reacle.*

The T's gone. It isn't smudged. It isn't torn. There's just a clean small gap where a letter used
to be, like a missing tooth.

“It was there on Sunday,” says Penny. “I checked. I always check.”

Somewhere behind us, in the dark at the edge of the light, the Guest takes another small bite of
parkin.

Somebody touches your elbow. It's Professor Momort. He's wrapped in a coat with too many collars,
and his face is lit orange on one side by the fire.

“Whatever you do,” he says, very low, “don't thank it. Not for anything. If it does you a kindness,
say *I'm grateful*. Never the other thing.”

“Why?”

He looks at the Guest for a long moment.

“Because the other thing is a door,” he says. “And you don't know what's on its side.”

Then a rocket goes up with a scream and bursts green over the stables, and when I look back, he's
gone.

## 3. Flat

**id** `november.scene.flat` · **When** Martinmas, Wed Nov 11 · **Room** the Great Hall, then the
Kitchens · **Cast** Serenity Brown, Ambrose Trencher, Pippa Pilcrow, the Guest

**Opening:** Supper's gone quiet. Nobody can taste it.

### Node `entry`

It's Martinmas, and the Great Hall's gone very quiet at supper.

There's goose. There's always goose at Martinmas, a big crackling brown one for every table, and
red cabbage, and roast potatoes with crunchy edges. It looks wonderful. It smells wonderful. The
whole Hall's full of the smell of it, warm and fat and gold.

Nobody can taste it.

You can see it go round the Hall, table by table. Somebody takes a bite and stops chewing. Somebody
else frowns at a potato as if it's told a lie. A first-year puts down his fork and says, loudly,
“Is it me?”

It isn't him. It's everybody.

Pippa stands up on her bench.

“It's the punctuation,” she says. “Somebody's taken the full stop off the gravy. Gravy without a
full stop just goes *on.*”

Nobody laughs. That's how I know it's bad.

At the end of the Hall, by the door to the Kitchens, the Guest is going round the tables with a
big jug of water, filling up glasses. Nobody asked it to. At every place, it stops for just a
moment, and leans a little over the plate, the way you'd lean over a flower.

Then it moves on. And the next plate goes flat.

Serenity's sitting near the window. She's got a plate of goose in front of her and she hasn't
touched it. She's watching the Hall. And then she starts to laugh.

It's a surprised laugh. It comes out of her before she can stop it, bright
and sudden, like a bird getting out of a box.

Everybody turns to look.

“Sorry,” she says. “Sorry. It's just—” She picks up her fork. She puts down her fork. “Now you
know.”

“Know what?” says Pippa.

“What it's like.” Serenity pushes her plate away an inch. “I haven't tasted anything since
October. Not since the Count. It all tastes like paper. I've been eating paper for six weeks and
smiling about it.”

The Hall's quieter than I've ever heard it. Even the fire's listening.

### Node `kitchens`

Later, in the Kitchens, Ambrose is halving loaves.

It's a Martinmas thing. Saint Martin was a soldier, in the old story, who met a beggar at a gate on
a freezing night and cut his own cloak in two with his sword to share it. So at Martinmas Ambrose
cuts every loaf in half, and keeps one half, and puts the other half in a basket by the door, for
whoever needs it.

He cuts one more than usual. He takes the extra half over to the stool by the fire and puts it in
the Guest's clean hands.

The Guest eats it. Ambrose watches.

Serenity's come down too. She's sitting on the end of the long table with her boots swinging, the
way she sits on everything, and she's watching the Guest as well. You've set me down beside her.

“It does it to every plate,” she says, quietly. “Have you seen? It leans over. Like it's smelling
them. And then they're gone.”

“Not yours,” I'd say, if I could say things to her. I can't. I press my corner against her map
case instead, and she looks down at me, and then at the Guest.

Because it's true. The Guest went round every place in the Hall. It leaned over every plate. When
it got to Serenity's, it leaned, and stopped, and its hood turned up towards her face for a long
moment.

Then it moved on. There was nothing on her plate for it to take.

Serenity turns her mug of tea round and round in her hands.

“It looked at me,” she says. “Like I was a locked door.”

## 4. The Rescue

**id** `november.scene.the-rescue` · **When** mid-month, about Sat Nov 14 · **Room** the Kitchens ·
**Cast** Ambrose Trencher, Penny Blackletter, Wicker Eddies, Soren Ng, Min-seo Kim, Serenity Brown,
the Guest

**Opening:** Penny's counted every missing letter. There's a pattern.

### Node `entry`

Penny's been up all night with *The Middlemost Cookery*.

She's got it open on the long table, with a lamp on each side and a magnifying glass and a stack of
index cards a hand high. She's gone through it page by page and written down every missing letter.
Her eyes are red. Her pencil's worn to a stub.

“Two hundred and fourteen,” she says. “Since Bonfire Night. The S's are going fastest. Then the
E's. The Christmas pudding's lost all its spices. The gingerbread's lost its *bread.*”

Ambrose stands at her shoulder. He's gone grey under his kitchen colour.

“Thirty years,” he says. “I've had that book thirty years.”

“There's something else.” Penny turns the pages back. “Look. Stone soup.”

The stone soup page is whole. Every letter's there, crisp and black. *Put a clean stone in the pot.
Tell everybody you're making soup.*

“You made it on Thursday,” says Penny. “For the second-years. And the soda bread, there, you made
on Tuesday. And the leek and potato. All whole.”

She sits back. She looks at Ambrose the way she looks at a piece of evidence that's finally decided
to speak.

“The only recipes keeping their letters,” she says, “are the ones somebody's making.”

The Kitchens go very quiet. By the fire, the Guest is scrubbing a pan that's already clean.

Ambrose ties his apron tighter.

“Then we cook,” he says.

### Node `cooking`

By the afternoon the Kitchens are full.

It's chaos. There are second-years making scones that could stop a door. There's a boy from the
Glint Hall crying over an onion, and he isn't sad, it's just the onion. Professor Boggle's making
soda bread with her sleeves pushed up to the elbow and flour in her eyebrows. Pippa's going round
correcting the recipe cards. “A *pinch*,” she's telling a first-year, “is not a measurement. It's
a threat.”

Ambrose stands in the middle of it all with a wooden spoon, saying “Lower the heat. Lower. Lower.
Good,” to everyone at once.

And Wicker Eddies is making an omelette.

He's never made an omelette. He's never made anything, as far as I know, unless you count trouble.
He's got the pan far too hot and he's got eggshell in it and he's frowning at it with the most
serious face I've ever seen him wear. He doesn't look at Serenity, at the far end of the table. He
looks at the pan as if the pan is a dare.

When it's done, it's brown on one side and runny on the other. He puts it on a plate. He walks it
down the whole length of the long table and sets it down in front of her, and walks away again
before she can say anything.

Serenity looks at the omelette. She takes a bite. She can't taste it. Everybody knows she can't.

She eats the whole thing anyway, slowly, without a word.

Soren's at the dresser, doing something nobody else has noticed. He's laying out teaspoons. Each
one's got a tiny taste on it: a grain of sea salt, a drop of honey, a curl of lemon peel, a smear
of mustard, a single blackcurrant. Beside each spoon there's a folded paper slip with a number on
it, one to nine.

When Serenity gets up to put her plate away, the first slip's sitting on her map case. It says,
in Soren's small careful hand: *Start at the dresser. Go in order. Don't skip seven.*

She stands there holding it. She doesn't open the rest. But she puts it in her pocket.

### Node `min-seo`

At the end of the afternoon, when the Kitchens have gone quiet and sticky, Min-seo Kim does
something nobody else has thought to do.

She goes and sits on the floor by the stool, beside the Guest. Not too close. Close enough.

“Can I ask you something?”

The hood turns.

“You've been here two weeks. Everybody's been talking about you. Nobody's asked you anything.” She
pulls her knees up. “What do you need?”

The Guest is quiet for a long time. The fire ticks. I'm holding my breath, as much as a book can.

“Something I used to have,” it says, at last.

“What was it?”

“I don't remember.” The clean hands turn over, palms up, empty. “That's why I'm eating. I'm
looking for it. I think it tasted of this house.”

Then Ambrose turns round from the range, and looks at you.

“Your kitchen counts too,” he says. “Whatever you make out there, where you live. It counts. A
recipe's only alive while somebody's making it. So make something.”

## 5. The Offer

**Opening:** It's three in the morning. Serenity can't sleep. The Guest is awake too.

### Node `entry`

Serenity comes down to the Kitchens at three in the morning.

She does that now. Since October, she hears things at night. Pages turning in the walls. Doors
that are really books. She can't sleep through it, so she walks, and her walks end up here, where
it's warm.

Ambrose has left something out for her. He always does now. Tonight it's a bowl of soup under a
plate, on the warm corner of the range, and a spoon laid next to it, and a folded napkin. He
doesn't say anything about it in the mornings. She doesn't either.

You're there because you couldn't sleep either. You're sitting on the bench by the dresser with me
on your knees. I've got my leaves shut. I keep them shut after dark now.

The Guest is awake.

It's at the sink, in its long green coat, scrubbing a pot. The pot's already clean. It scrubs it
anyway, slowly, round and round, the way you'd stroke a dog. In the low light from the range, I can
see that the sleeves of the coat are far too long for it. It's rolled them up four times.

Serenity sits down with her soup. She can't taste it. She eats it anyway.

The Guest stops scrubbing.

“I could give it back,” it says.

Serenity's spoon stops halfway.

It's very quiet. Even the walls have stopped turning pages.

At night the Guest doesn't smell of nothing any more. It smells of frost. Frost and old iron, like
a gate in winter. I didn't notice it till now. I wish I hadn't.

“What for?” says Serenity.

Now it turns. The hood tilts towards her.

“One evening,” it says. “You've got one I'd like. An evening in November.”

## 6. Stir-up Sunday

**id** `november.scene.stir-up-sunday` · **When** Sun Nov 22 · **Room** the Kitchens · **Cast**
Ambrose Trencher, the Guest, Headmistress Thorne (glimpsed), the whole Academy

**Opening:** Everybody stirs the pudding. Everybody makes a wish.

### Node `entry`

It's Stir-up Sunday, the last Sunday before Advent, and the whole Academy's queueing out of the
Kitchens and halfway up the stairs.

That's the custom. Ambrose makes the Christmas pudding today, in the biggest bowl in the building,
a great brown earthenware one he can barely lift. And everybody in the house has to take a turn at
the stirring. You stir from east to west, the way the three kings travelled in the old story. You
shut your eyes. And you make a wish, and you don't say it out loud, or it won't come.

The pudding's nearly whole again. Ambrose made it on Thursday as a test, to keep the page alive,
and he's making it now for real. The spices have come back to the recipe, letter by letter, as the
Academy's cooked. Penny's been counting. One hundred and twelve letters restored. Some of them came
from out there, from people's own kitchens, and Ambrose says that's the strangest and best part.

The queue moves. Pippa stirs and wishes with her whole face screwed up. Vellum stirs exactly three
times and makes a note. Serenity stirs, and laughs at something, and won't say what.

You stir. I can't stir. I haven't got hands. But you hold me over the bowl while you do it, and I
make a wish too. I'm not telling you what it is either. Those are the rules.

Then the Guest steps up.

Nobody's sure it's allowed. Everybody looks at Ambrose. Ambrose holds out the wooden spoon.

The Guest takes it in both its clean hands. It stirs from east to west, slowly, round and round.
It shuts its eyes, under the hood.

And then it does what you must never do. It says its wish out loud.

“Home,” it says. “The smell of the house when the bowl was put down. I want to go home.”

The spoon stops.

At the back of the Kitchens, in the doorway to the stairs, there's a tall dark shape in a long
black coat. It's Headmistress Thorne. I didn't hear her come. She's standing perfectly still with
her hands folded, and she's watching the Guest, and her face is doing nothing at all.

Then she turns and goes back up the stairs, and doesn't come into the room.

She hasn't come into a room the Guest's been in all month. I've only just noticed.

### Node `the-page`

That night, after the queue's gone and the pudding's steaming in its cloth, Ambrose sits down at
the long table with *The Middlemost Cookery*.

He doesn't open it at a recipe. He opens it at the back, at the place he always opens it, where
the endpaper's coming loose. For thirty years he's been trying to read what's under that endpaper.
There's a page stuck to it. A page with writing on it that nobody's been able to make out.

Tonight you can read it.

Not all of it. The Academy's cooking has brought back most of the letters. The title's whole now,
in old brown handwriting:

*A Bowl for the One Who Keeps the Pots.*

Under it there's a list of things. Most of it's there. Some of it isn't. There are gaps, like
missing teeth.

Ambrose reads it three times. Then he puts his hand flat on the page, the way you'd put your hand
on a sleeping animal to feel it breathe.

“That's it,” he says. “That's what I've been looking for.”

He looks across at the stool by the fire. The Guest's asleep there, in its green coat, with its
sleeves rolled up four times.

“It's not a recipe for us,” he says. “It's for them. It always was.”
