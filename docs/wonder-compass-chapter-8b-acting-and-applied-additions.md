## Acting and adjacent research examined

### Findings used in the chapter

- **Role-play and persistence:** White and colleagues' [Batman experiment](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12695) supplies the child example in the stuck-decision section.
- **Participation and remembering:** Banducci and colleagues' [active-experiencing study](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnagi.2017.00133) supplies the adult acting example and informs goal-directed character work. Its allocation and follow-up limitations are recorded in the bundled notes.
- **An object as an audience:** Asaba and colleagues' [puppet experiments](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogdev.2022.101186) supply the opening example. The comparison involves a social setup, including question wording, rather than an isolated effect of calling something a friend.

### Supporting context in the bundled notes

- Brown, Cockett, and Yuan's [2019 acting fMRI study](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.181908) supports investigating role adoption as a distinct task. Neural differences are not a demonstrated benefit or an explanation of every character exercise.
- Stengelin and colleagues' [2023 registered puppet report](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13913) helps distinguish social participation from equating a puppet with a human.
- Goldstein and Lerner's [dramatic-play trial](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.12603), Chu and colleagues' [2026 acting review](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1749252), and Smits-van der Nat and colleagues' [2026 pretend-play intervention](https://doi.org/10.1111/sode.70050) keep the account specific about outcomes. The chapter makes no universal empathy or intelligence claim.

### Further lead retained outside the manuscript

Greaves and colleagues (2022), [“Exploring Theater Neuroscience”](https://doi.org/10.1162/jocn_a_01912), used wearable fNIRS with six actors rehearsing Shakespeare. The measured prefrontal response to hearing one's own name was suppressed during rehearsal. This is a small methods demonstration of self-related attention in performance, not evidence that the self disappears or that talking to objects produces the same effect. The [author-hosted paper](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10158535/1/Greaves%26Pinti_etal.%2C%282022%29_jocn_a_01912.pdf) is available for a future acting-focused section.

The earlier research record retains other candidates, including multimedia learning and social assurance from products. Those findings were not added to this chapter: the strongest examples already serve its argument, and more studies would interrupt its pace.
