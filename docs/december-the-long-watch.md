# The Long Watch — December 2026 story

**Draft 1, 2026-11-16, for bj to read.** Nothing here is built, staged, signed or published.
Theme confirmed by bj 2026-11-16 ([faerie-tale-spell-plan.md](faerie-tale-spell-plan.md), Phase 9).
Scenes: [december-the-long-watch-scenes.md](december-the-long-watch-scenes.md).

**Thesis:** you're safe on the longest night if you're known by name and somebody's watching for
you. **The month's question:** who sits up with you through the longest night?

October asked who may be invited across a threshold. November asked what you owe them once they're
inside. December asks who stays awake with you when it's dark for longest, and who you stay awake
for.

---

## The Hunt

**What's coming.** At midwinter the Wild Hunt rides. This year it rides with the Unseelie Court,
the same Court the Guest ate at and served (November). The Hunt is horses and hounds and riders
going over in the dark, and the old stories agree about one thing: it takes along whoever it finds
out on the road with nobody watching for them. You don't come back from riding with it. You just
keep riding.

**What turns it.** Two old protections, and the whole month rides on them:

1. **A light in the window.** It says somebody in here is awake and watching for whoever's out
   there.
2. **Everyone inside known by name.** The Hunt passes over a house where every soul under the
   roof has a name the house knows. It stops at one where something doesn't.

**The trouble with names.** The Book has read the other law too: whoever knows your name has power
over you (Rumpelstiltskin; the secret-name spell already in the grimoire). So being named protects
you in one story and exposes you in another, and on the longest night nobody's sure which story
they're in. That's the month's quiet engine, and the Book says it plainly in scene 1: "I've read
both. I don't know which one's true tonight."

**The Roll of the House.** On Thorne's order Professor Boggle takes the Roll: every person, and
every thing that's become somebody, under the Academy's roof, read out in the Great Hall and
answered. Boggle keeps the rooms. She knows which stairs creak and which chimneys need a word, so
she's the one who can tell when the house is short.

**Something is missing from the Roll**, and what it is depends on how November ended
(`recalls` on Ambrose's `the-guest.bowl.*` memories):

| November ending | What's missing from the Roll | Why it matters on the longest night |
|---|---|---|

Something under the roof isn't on the Roll. |

The watch tag the Reader chose on the first of December (`december-watch-ambrose` / `-light` /
`-house`) decides who's already keeping watch when the trouble's found, and colours three scenes.
It never gates anything.

**The Reader's own Roll.** In scene 4 Boggle hands the Reader a blank roll card: who lives in your
house that isn't a person? This is the Neighbours (Phase 1 of the spell plan): the tree with a name,
the kettle that sulks, the heron by the weir. The Reader names one thing in one sentence. If the
Reader's Book already has Neighbours, the Book reads them back off its own Roll. The Book asks to be
put on the Reader's Roll too.

**The Watch.** On the solstice (Mon 21 December 2026, the longest night) the Academy puts every
lamp out but the window lights and sits up. The Reader is asked to do it too, for real: lights off
for a while on the longest night, and one sentence about what kept watch with them.

**The Huntsman.** He stops at the lit window and asks for a riddle game, the way Odin did at King
Heidrek's hall disguised as Gestumblindi. His last riddle is the one in every riddle contest that
only the asker could answer: *What's my name?* He doesn't know any more. He's been riding too long.
(King Herla, in Walter Map's twelfth-century book, went to a faerie wedding, stayed what felt like
three days, came home two hundred years late, and was told not to get down from his horse. He's
still riding.)

**The Twelve Nights.** In parts of Germany and Austria the twelve nights of midwinter (the
*Rauhnächte*) are when the Hunt is abroad, and in some places they begin on St Thomas's night, the
longest one. So from the solstice the Book counts twelve nights, and on each one a Neighbour speaks.
Things are said to talk at midwinter: the animals in the stable at midnight on Christmas Eve, in
the old European belief Hardy wrote "The Oxen" about. The Book thinks the kettle's got just as much
right.

**Games are how the night's passed.** Riddles at the range (the Exeter Book riddles, a thousand
years old; Pippa's punctuation riddles), forfeits in the Great Hall after Christmas, ghost stories,
and the long table. A long night is survived by company and noise, which is also what wassailing is.

**Wassail.** On New Year's Eve the Academy wassails the oldest apple tree in the orchard: toast in
the branches, cider on the roots, a racket to send off whatever's in the dark, and a promise to the
tree. In the cider counties (Somerset, Devon, Herefordshire) it's done on Twelfth Night or Old
Twelfth Night, and it's a deal: we look after you, you give us apples. The Reader makes a deal with
a tree of their own, in one sentence, and the Book comes back for it (the deals of Phase 0).

## How it ends

The climax is the solstice watch (scene 8). The Hunt passes over. The Huntsman stops at the lit
window and plays riddles. Whatever's missing from the Roll is kept, in every ending, because
somebody sat up. **The Reader's last choice is what to do with the Huntsman's last riddle, and
none of the three punishes them.**

| Ending | The Reader's choice | What follows |
|---|---|---|
| **Riddled** | Answer with a riddle of your own: *What's under this roof?* ("Everybody. By name.") | He laughs, which nobody's heard the Hunt do. The Hunt rides on. In the morning there are hoofprints in the frost that come up to the window and stop. |
| **Named** | Give him a name for the road. | He takes it like a coat. He rides on with it. Nobody knows if a name changes anything for a rider who can't get down. The Court finds out in February. |
| **Watched** | Say nothing. Keep watching. Keep the light lit. | He waits the whole night at the window, and you wait with him, and at first light he isn't there. Nobody played. Nobody lost. |

Outcomes recorded for January and February to read: `december:hunt-riddled`,
`december:hunt-named`, `december:hunt-watched`.

In every ending, on the morning of the 22nd, Thorne comes in from outside at dawn with frost on
her hem. Nobody asks where she's been.

## Thorne, kept ambiguous

Thorne orders the Roll. **Whether that's protection (known by name, so the Hunt passes over) or a
count for the Court (known by name, so it can be claimed) is never settled.** She's absent from the
Great Hall during the Watch. Her window in the east tower is lit all night. At dawn she comes in
from outside. In the Named ending the Huntsman bows towards the east tower before he rides on, or
he's looking at the dawn. The Book can't tell. February's Thorned Bargain tilts it.

## Folklore under it (said as folklore, attributed)

- The Wild Hunt. Jacob Grimm gathered it as the *Wilde Jagd*. Odin or Wodan leads it in German and
  Scandinavian tellings; Gwyn ap Nudd and the hounds of Annwn in Wales; Herne in Shakespeare's
  *Merry Wives of Windsor*; King Herla's household in Walter Map.
- The Peterborough Chronicle, 1127: huntsmen seen in the deer park and the woods, black and big
  and loathsome, on black horses, with black hounds.
- Gabriel hounds (northern England): the calls of wild geese going over at night, heard as the
  Hunt's dogs.
- The *Rauhnächte*: twelve midwinter nights when the Hunt is abroad. Don't hang washing out, or
  the Hunt takes it. In some places they start on St Thomas's night, 21 December.
- A candle in the window on Christmas Eve, to welcome whoever's on the road (Irish custom).
- St Lucy's Day, 13 December, the old shortest day before the calendar changed in 1752: "Lucy
  light, the shortest day and the longest night." Donne's "A Nocturnal upon S. Lucy's Day."
- Animals kneel or speak at midnight on Christmas Eve (European folk belief; Hardy, "The Oxen").
- Riddle contests: Odin as Gestumblindi against King Heidrek (*Hervarar saga*), ending on a
  question only the asker could answer. The Exeter Book riddles.
- Forfeits and parlour games at Christmas.
- Wassailing the apple trees: toast in the branches, cider on the roots, noise; Twelfth Night or
  Old Twelfth Night (17 January) in the cider counties.
- True names (Rumpelstiltskin) against being known by name (the Roll). Both are folklore. The Roll
  of the House is the Academy's own.

## Laws for this story

- Thorne's knowledge stays ambiguous. Never resolved in December.
- The triangle is never settled. Serenity chooses.
- The Huntsman is never only a villain: he was somebody once, and he plays fair.
- The Hunt never takes anybody. The Reader is never punished by an ending.
- Nobody is ever told they were somewhere they weren't. Lines about November go through a recall
  with a fallback.
- The Book has no hands. The Reader carries it. It never calls itself "the Book".
- No "hinge", no "ritual", no "forgive" in the margins.
- The Book says which parts are folklore, which are the Academy's, and which are the Reader's.
- Not gentle. The longest night is dark and long, and the Book says so. Nobody lowers the lamps
  to soothe anybody: they put them out because that's the Watch.
- Scenes stand alone for a Reader who dips in.
