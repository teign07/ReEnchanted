# Chapter 8B: animist perspectives, anthropomorphism, and childhood research

Research completed October 22, 2026. **Selected findings have now been applied to Chapter 8B and its bundled Notes & References.** The recommendations and sketches below record the original research proposal; they are not a verbatim copy of the final additions. See [the applied additions and acting research](wonder-compass-chapter-8b-acting-and-applied-additions.md) for the implemented scope, further research, and validation.

## Editorial assessment

There is a substantial foundation for the chapter beyond a narrow account of anthropomorphism as an agency-detection error. The strongest additions concern relational ways of knowing, the selective attachment children develop toward particular things, imaginative engagement with intact reality discrimination, and specific behavioral effects of character framing.

Three distinctions matter:

- **Animist perspectives** are diverse cultural, religious, and practical relationships with other-than-human beings. They are not interchangeable with an experimental instruction to imagine that a toaster is grumpy.
- **Anthropomorphism** attributes human-like qualities to nonhumans. Some relational perspectives instead attend to a being's particular capacities and relationships, without making it a miniature human.
- **Anthropocentrism** uses humans as the central model or reference point. A relational or ecocentric perspective can resist anthropocentrism even while recognizing nonhuman agency.

The evidence supports a confident invitation to attend, relate, and play. It does not establish that adopting an animist perspective causes better health, that all Indigenous communities share one worldview, or that a special “animist brain” mechanism validates the Compass.

## Strongest cultural and psychological foundations

### 1. Knowing through relationship: Bird-David (1999)

Nurit Bird-David's ethnography with Nayaka people in southern India describes a relational way of knowing one's environment. Her discussion of talking with trees concerns sustained attention to changing relationships and responses, rather than simply assigning a hidden human mind to a tree. This is an anthropological interpretation grounded in fieldwork, not a clinical outcome study.

**Fit:** Strengthens “Why Your Ancestors Talked to Trees” and “It Breaks the Utilitarian Trance.” It gives the attention-and-care argument a cultural foundation in its own right.

Bird-David, N. (1999). “‘Animism’ revisited: Personhood, environment, and relational epistemology.” *Current Anthropology, 40*(S1), S67–S91. https://doi.org/10.1086/200061. [Accessible paper](https://lchc.ucsd.edu/MCA/Mail/xmcamail.2012_08.dir/pdfFNa83UDbvD.pdf).

### 2. Agency need not mean human psychology: ojalehto, Medin, and García (2017)

Two experiments with Ngöbe adults in Panama and US college students found systematic differences in how participants understood and attributed agency. Ngöbe explanations emphasized relational capacities and directed interactions; US explanations more often required human-like consciousness. Relational framing facilitated Ngöbe agency judgments. The work included a coauthor from the Ngöbe Culture and Language Education Program and examined variation within as well as between groups.

**Fit:** Supports adding a second route into the practice: asking how something participates in a place, rather than always inventing human-like wants for it. The comparison does not establish a hierarchy of cultures or an animism-to-health causal effect.

ojalehto, b. l., Medin, D. L., & García, S. (2017). “Grounding principles for inferring agency: Two cultural perspectives.” *Cognitive Psychology, 95*, 50–78. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2017.04.001. [Author manuscript](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/medin/documents/OjalehtoMedinGarcia2017CogPsychAM.pdf).

### 3. Cultural perspectives organize attention and ecological reasoning: Bang, Medin, and Atran (2007)

Comparisons of Menominee and European American children and adults in rural Wisconsin found differences in how ecological knowledge was organized and brought to mind. Ecological relationships appeared earlier in the Menominee children's explanations. Findings with adult fish experts also showed that task prompts could reduce some group differences, suggesting differences in habitual organization rather than a simple knowledge deficit.

**Fit:** Supports the idea that how we habitually approach the world affects the relationships we notice. It does not isolate animism from experience, culture, education, or community practice.

Bang, M., Medin, D. L., & Atran, S. (2007). “Cultural mosaics and mental models of nature.” *PNAS, 104*(35), 13868–13874. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0706627104. [Author-hosted paper](https://education.uw.edu/sites/default/files/Bang%20Medin%20Atran%202007%20Cultural%20Mosiacs.pdf).

### 4. Anthropomorphism as interaction, with accurate knowledge intact: Airenti (2018)

Airenti proposes that anthropomorphism can be an interactional stance: people put a nonhuman entity in the position of an interlocutor while retaining their ordinary knowledge about it. The same person can alternate between a mechanical description and playful conversation. This is a theoretical synthesis of developmental and interaction research, not a new intervention trial.

**Fit:** Especially useful for the Permission section and for protecting the chapter's playfulness. One clear explanation of the imaginative frame can do its work without repeatedly interrupting each character's dialogue.

Airenti, G. (2018). “The development of anthropomorphism in interaction: Intersubjectivity, imagination, and theory of mind.” *Frontiers in Psychology, 9*, 2136. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02136.

## Childhood findings worth using

### 5. Children selectively attribute minds to emotionally significant toys: Gjersoe, Hall, and Hood (2015)

Young children were more likely to attribute mental states to attachment toys than to other favorite toys. Attachment combined with personifying features mattered; familiarity or a face alone did not explain the pattern. This was selective, rather than an indiscriminate belief that every toy had a mind. The studies do not show that naming a new object produces attachment or improves well-being.

**Fit:** A direct foundation for the chapter's special mug, old coat, and familiar possessions. Their history with the reader is more relevant than a blanket claim that the brain treats every object as a friend.

Gjersoe, N. L., Hall, E. L., & Hood, B. (2015). “Children attribute mental lives to toys when they are emotionally attached to them.” *Cognitive Development, 34*, 28–38. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogdev.2014.12.002. [Institutional record and abstract](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/children-attribute-mental-lives-to-toys-when-they-are-emotionally/).

### 6. Imaginary companions do not require poorer reality discrimination: Taylor, Cartwright, and Carlson (1993)

In a small study of 27 four-year-olds, children with and without imaginary companions did not differ on fantasy–reality discrimination. Companion descriptions were followed up about seven months later. The finding supports coexistence of imaginative involvement and reality discrimination; its small sample does not establish that all children always distinguish fantasy and reality.

**Fit:** A short permission-giving example. Avoid implying that the benefit depends on confusing the two.

Taylor, M., Cartwright, B. S., & Carlson, S. M. (1993). “A developmental investigation of children's imaginary companions.” *Developmental Psychology, 29*(2), 276–285. https://doi.org/10.1037/0012-1649.29.2.276. [Full paper](https://www.csueastbay.edu/psychology/files/docs/articles/developmental-investigation-of-imaginary-friends.pdf).

### 7. The Batman effect: a character frame changed persistence

In an experiment with 180 four- and six-year-olds, children who impersonated an exemplary character persisted longer at a repetitive task than children using a first-person perspective, with third-person self-reference intermediate. An attractive video game was available as a distraction.

**Fit:** Useful support for the broader proposition that a playful change in perspective can change behavior. It is a **related mechanism**, not a test of anthropomorphizing shoes, adult decision fatigue, or the Compass sequence.

White, R. E., Prager, E. O., Schaefer, C., Kross, E., Duckworth, A. L., & Carlson, S. M. (2017). “The ‘Batman effect’: Improving perseverance in young children.” *Child Development, 88*(5), 1563–1571. https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12695. [Author-hosted paper](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2017/09/White_et_al-2017-Child_Development.pdf).

### 8. Some benefits of dramatic play have experimental support

A randomized study of 97 four-year-olds from low-income backgrounds found specific improvements in emotional control after dramatic pretend-play games. It did not find improvements across all measured social skills, including empathy and theory of mind.

Goldstein, T. R., & Lerner, M. D. (2018). “Dramatic pretend play games uniquely improve emotional control in young children.” *Developmental Science, 21*(4), e12603. https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.12603. [Full paper](https://www.aep-arts.org/wp-content/uploads/Dramatic-Pretend-Play-Games-Uniquely-Improve-Emotional-Control-in-Young-Children.pdf).

**Fit:** Optional notes evidence that particular imaginative practices can have measurable outcomes. A lower-priority body addition because it is less directly about objects.

### 9. Current pretend-play evidence is encouraging but outcome-specific

A 2024 meta-analysis of 34 studies found a positive association between pretend play and social competence in children aged 3–8; most studies were cross-sectional. A 2026 eight-week intervention with 333 children in 15 randomly assigned schools found no significant effect on its social-cognitive or social-emotional outcomes. Together these findings support specificity rather than “pretending makes children smarter or more empathetic.”

Smits-van der Nat, M., van der Wilt, F., Meeter, M., & van der Veen, C. (2024). “The value of pretend play for social competence in early childhood: A meta-analysis.” *Educational Psychology Review, 36*, 46. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-024-09884-z.

The same authors (2026). “The effect of pretend play on children's social competence: An intervention study in early childhood education.” *Social Development, 35*(2), e70050. https://doi.org/10.1111/sode.70050.

### 10. A current outdoor-kindergarten study closely fits the book's concerns

An ethnographic study in Norway examines three encounters involving children's spontaneous personification and adults' guidance. The cases show both caring engagement and mistaken interpretations of what living things need. It is qualitative work illustrating possibilities and dilemmas, not evidence of a measured intervention effect.

**Fit:** Supports “let the character lead you into learning what this being actually needs.” Worth a notes reference; lower priority for a new anecdote in the already long chapter.

Brandt, L. A., Menning, S. F., & Fasting, M. L. (2026). “She is so cute. I love her. — Exploring ecological literacy through acts of spontaneous anthropomorphism in kindergarten.” *Journal of Early Childhood Literacy*, advance online publication, September 10. https://doi.org/10.1177/14687984261487658.

## Neuroscience: what it adds

### 11. Social interpretation can recruit mentalizing regions without human bodies

- Castelli, F., Happé, F., Frith, U., & Frith, C. (2000). “Movement and mind: A functional imaging study of perception and interpretation of complex intentional movement patterns.” *NeuroImage, 12*(3), 314–325. https://doi.org/10.1006/nimg.2000.0612. **PET, not fMRI**; six adults. Intentional interpretations of moving shapes engaged regions implicated in mentalizing.
- Krach, S., et al. (2008). “Can machines think? Interaction and perspective taking with robots investigated via fMRI.” *PLOS ONE, 3*(7), e2597. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0002597. Twenty participants played a game with putative computer, robot, and human partners; responses were secretly random. More human-like partners were associated with greater mentalizing-region activity and reported engagement.

**Fit:** The brain explanation can remain positive and concrete: humans can bring social interpretation to nonhuman encounters. These studies do not establish sentience, identical relationships, or improved judgment, and their small samples and task contexts matter.

### 12. This can support the later DMN redemption

Mars, R. B., et al. (2012). “On the relationship between the ‘default mode network’ and the ‘social brain.’” *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 6*, 189. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2012.00189. The paper discusses overlap and distinctions between systems associated with internally oriented thought and social cognition.

**Fit:** Supports continuity with the later positive account of imagination and social thinking. It gives no basis for saying that character play switches the DMN off.

No direct evidence identified here demonstrates that adopting an animist worldview creates a particular beneficial neural profile. The 2025 Nature perspective by Illes and colleagues is relevant background on integrating Indigenous perspectives into neuroscience, **not a neuroimaging demonstration of animist benefits**: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08437-2.

## Two additional psychological findings

### 13. Actual learning outcomes from anthropomorphic materials

A 2024 meta-analysis identified 33 experiments from 13 papers on adding facial features to multimedia learning materials. Pooled outcomes favored anthropomorphic materials for retention, transfer, and comprehension. This supports specific learning-design applications, rather than the removed claim that naming any gadget makes its owner smarter or more technically competent.

Liu, K., & Su, P. (2024). “Effectiveness of facial anthropomorphism design for improving multimedia learning outcomes: Systematic review and meta-analysis.” *Smart Learning Environments, 11*, 42. https://doi.org/10.1186/s40561-024-00332-7.

**Fit:** Strong notes evidence and a possible single body sentence if a learning claim is wanted. Avoid assuming that observed learning improvements prove a particular attention mechanism.

### 14. Social assurance from products: useful but mixed implications

Four experiments found that anthropomorphic products changed compensatory responses after social exclusion. Outcomes included less exaggeration of social connections, less anticipated contact with close others, and less willingness to engage in prosocial behavior. The authors interpret this as partial fulfillment of social assurance needs. Explicitly highlighting the product's nonliving status eliminated the effect in their boundary test.

**Fit:** Directly relevant to the possibility of nonhuman social comfort, but **not a clean “makes life better” result**. It did not establish lasting relief from chronic loneliness, and reduced motivation to seek human contact matters. This supports preserving the chapter's bridge back into actual life. The reminder manipulation is not a reason to withhold an accurate explanation from readers; the editorial inference is simply to explain the frame clearly and then allow the game to breathe.

Mourey, J. A., Olson, J. G., & Yoon, C. (2017). “Products as pals: Engaging with anthropomorphic products mitigates the effects of social exclusion.” *Journal of Consumer Research, 44*(2), 414–431. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucx038. [Author-hosted full paper](https://www.jimmourey.com/_files/ugd/e0b202_6cb58ef7aeda4b10a299591be1ae713e.pdf).

## Findings that prevent misleading childhood claims

- Tahiroglu and Taylor (2019), https://doi.org/10.1111/bjdp.12272: in 264 college participants and 73 children, anthropomorphism was not strongly associated with general social understanding/theory of mind. Imaginary-companion history was associated with anthropomorphism. Do not claim a general empathy or mind-reading advantage.
- Herrmann, Waxman, and Medin (2010), https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1004440107: three-year-olds in their biological-induction task did not show the human-centered pattern seen in urban five-year-olds. This is about anthropocentric biological reasoning, not proof that three-year-olds never personify anything. It also challenges a universal progression from primitive animism to mature rationality.
- Woolley and Ghossainy (2013), https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12081: the developmental literature includes substantial skepticism and reality evaluation in children, alongside credulity. Avoid treating childhood imagination as evidence of blanket reality confusion.

## Original recommendations and voice sketches

1. **One relational paragraph** under “Why Your Ancestors Talked to Trees,” grounded in Bird-David and ojalehto. Present a way of meeting and learning a place, not a promise that adopting another community's worldview brings measured health benefits.
2. **One childhood paragraph** in “The Permission,” using selective attachment and intact reality discrimination. It should invite play without diagnosing it or asserting a general intelligence benefit.
3. **A compact Batman example** under the stuck-decision discussion, explicitly about what children did in that experiment. Keep application to adult missions as the field-guide invitation.
4. Put neuroscience detail, the learning meta-analysis, current mixed childhood outcomes, and any social-assurance qualification primarily in the notes. Do not add another long scientific detour.

Possible voice sketches, **not applied text**:

> Some ways of knowing begin by returning. The same tree. Different weather. New birds in the branches. You learn who lives there, what changes, what your presence does. Give a place that kind of attention and it stops being scenery. You have a history together.

> You can know exactly what a bear is stuffed with and still tuck him in. The imagination can join in while your knowledge stays put. Let your old coat have an opinion about the weather. Then take both of you outside and find out.

> In one experiment, children taking on a character like Batman stuck with a dull job longer. A little make-believe reached all the way into what they did next. Give your next small task a character and see what happens at the door.

These are proposed authorial applications of the research, not quotations from the papers or claims that an adult Compass mission has already been tested.
