## Best addition: noticing ordinary nature and feeling connected

[Passmore & Holder (2017), “Noticing nature: Individual and social benefits of a two-week intervention,” The Journal of Positive Psychology, 12(6), 537–546](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439760.2016.1221126).

In a randomized study of 395 undergraduates, participants attended to emotional responses to everyday nature, photographed things that evoked emotion, and described those feelings. Comparison groups attended to human-built surroundings or continued as usual. The nature group reported greater positive affect, a broader sense of connection to nature, people and life, and prosocial orientation after two weeks. These were self-reported outcomes, not evidence of lasting recovery from loneliness.

[Passmore, Yang & Sabine (2022), “An Extended Replication Study of the Well-Being Intervention, the Noticing Nature Intervention (NNI),” Journal of Happiness Studies, 23, 2663–2683](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10902-022-00516-3), randomized 173 undergraduates in China to a modified noticing practice or two active controls. It found better wellbeing and lower ill-being; the nature group did not spend significantly more time outdoors. That supports the emphasis on attending differently during ordinary routines, rather than requiring a special expedition. It does not isolate attention from every other component of the activity.

**Placement:** After “This is the secret physics of gathering: connecting with the world gives you something to share with people.” This is unusually close to the chapter's notice–feel–capture practice. It supports that practical route without establishing the three kinds of lonely as a clinical taxonomy.

**Possible short passage:**

> In one two-week study, people noticed ordinary bits of nature, photographed what stirred a feeling, and described it. They ended up feeling more connected—to nature, to other people, to life itself—than people in the comparison groups. A leaf had more to offer than it looked like.

The original publisher abstract was accessible; its full text returned an access error. The replication's publisher abstract and research details were accessible. Keep any final wording within those verified findings.

## Best addition near the Quick Win: a small reach-out matters

[Liu, Rim, Min & Min, “The Surprise of Reaching Out: Appreciated More Than We Think,” Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 124(4), 754–771](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000402), first published online in 2022, print issue 2023. The [publisher-hosted full paper](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-pspi0000402.pdf) was accessible.

Across preregistered experiments, initiators underestimated recipients' appreciation of brief messages and small gifts. This is a direct fit for “I noticed this and thought of you.” It concerns appreciation of appropriate, friendly contact within a social circle, rather than guaranteed welcome from every person or proof of a durable reduction in loneliness.

**Placement:** Immediately before the Quick Win, leaving its existing instruction and alternative for someone without a person to text intact.

**Possible short passage:**

> Experiments on little messages and small gifts found that people appreciated being remembered more than the sender expected. That “thought of you” text can carry more than you think.

## Strong support for the notes: open attention and loneliness

[Lindsay, Young, Brown, Smyth & Creswell (2019), “Mindfulness training reduces loneliness and increases social contact in a randomized controlled trial,” PNAS, 116(9), 3488–3493](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1813588116). [Author-hosted full paper](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/2019_LindsayWarrenBrown_PNAS.pdf).

A randomized trial with 153 adults compared 14 smartphone lessons in attention plus acceptance, attention alone, and an active control. The combined training reduced daily loneliness and increased social contact relative to both comparisons. Attention alone did not produce these benefits. The assessed period was short; this was a structured mindfulness intervention, not a test of the Compass.

This supports a useful ingredient: returning to experience with openness, including room for unpleasant feelings. It strengthens “You can’t shame yourself into feeling less lonely” and connects with Chapter 4B's existing permission paragraph. The book already cites the team's 2018 positive-affect report; this 2019 loneliness report uses the same 153-person trial, so describe it as an additional outcome, not an independent replication. Keep it in Chapter 3's notes rather than adding another research paragraph.

## Optional support for “an honest question is a gift”

[Huang et al. (2017), “It Doesn’t Hurt to Ask: Question-Asking Increases Liking,” JPSP, 113(3), 430–452](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000097), included experiments where people instructed to ask more questions were better liked and perceived as more responsive. Its experimental evidence fits the bakery invitation. The speed-dating component was observational and drew a methodological debate; it is unnecessary to the recommendation.

The [2025 published correction](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40111841/) reports minor reporting errors found by an independent audit and says the conclusions remained valid. Cite the correction alongside the original if adding this reference. No new body paragraph is needed: the existing commuter experiment already serves the social invitation well.
